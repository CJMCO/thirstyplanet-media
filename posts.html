POSTS

Every number, one everyday thing at a time.

Water hides. It hides in your t-shirt, in your breakfast, in the phone in your pocket. Almost none of it is the water you can see, and that is what makes it interesting.

So the posts here go looking for it, in a few different ways. Some pick up one everyday thing and ask a simple question: how much water did it take to make this? Some take a strange word that the water industry uses all day long and turn it into normal language. Some test the things everyone says about water, to find out which ones are actually true. Some travel to places that nearly ran dry, and ask how they got out of it. And some look at whole industries, the factories behind your clothes, your food, your paper.

Pick any one below. Every post gives you the number and the story behind it, and says where the number came from.

Foundations

Start here if you are new. From space the Earth looks blue, but almost all of that blue is salt water. Most of the fresh water is frozen solid or sitting too deep underground to reach. What is left over for every person, every farm and every factory is a very small slice of the planet, and we all share it. These posts lay out that picture, along with the idea that runs underneath everything else here: the water you drink in a day is tiny next to the water you use without ever seeing it.

Thirsty: ___

Pick up something ordinary. A t-shirt, a cup of coffee, a bar of chocolate, a sheet of paper. Now ask how much water it took for that thing to exist at all. The answer is almost never inside the object. It is out in a cotton field, on a coffee farm, in a forest planted to be turned into pulp, usually thousands of kilometres from you. One post, one object, one number, and the number always comes with its source. Fair warning: the t-shirt you are wearing holds about three years of drinking water.

Plain Water: ___

The water industry talks in code. COD, sludge, effluent, ETP. These words decide whether a river stays alive and how much a factory pays at the end of the month, and almost nobody outside the industry is ever told what they mean. Each post takes one word and explains it the way you would explain it to a friend at dinner: what it is, why anyone bothers to measure it, and what actually happens when it goes wrong. No exam at the end.

Myth: ___

People repeat a lot of things about water. That bottled is cleaner than tap. That wipes really are flushable. That the ice melting will leave us with plenty. Some of it is true, some is half true, and some survives only because it sounds sensible. Each post takes one claim and checks it properly, against evidence rather than opinion. The honest answer is often that it depends, and what it depends on usually turns out to be the interesting part.

Thirsty Places: ___

Running out of water is much easier to picture when it happens somewhere real, to real people. Singapore had almost no fresh water of its own and built its way out, to the point of drinking water it had already used once. The Aral Sea was drained to grow cotton and mostly disappeared within a single lifetime, leaving fishing boats sitting in sand. These posts follow what went wrong, what people did about it, and what the rest of us can copy.

Thirsty Industries: ___

Behind everything you buy there is a factory, and factories drink. Clothes, food and drink, paper, the chip in your phone. This is the world I work in every day, so these posts go past the shocking number and into the useful part: where the water actually goes once it is inside a plant, which fixes already exist, and what they cost. One example to keep: in most food factories, most of the water never touches the food. It washes the machines.

Inside: ___ Coming

Reading about a dyehouse is one thing. Standing inside one, with the noise and the steam and the colour running off the cloth into a drain, is another thing entirely. This is the part that needs a camera instead of a keyboard. Visits to real plants, filmed on site, with the people who run them showing you their own machines and explaining what each one does. It starts as soon as the first shoot is done.
