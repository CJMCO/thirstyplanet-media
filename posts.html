Foundations

Before any of the other numbers mean anything, a few things have to be in place: how much fresh water the planet actually has, who uses it, and why the water you can see coming out of a tap is the smallest part of the story. These are the posts that set that up, including the very first one published here. If you are new, start with them.

Thirsty: ___

The series that started everything. It takes one ordinary object, a t-shirt, a cup of coffee, a loaf of bread, and asks how much water it took to make. The answer is almost never inside the object. It is in a cotton field, a rice paddy, a dairy farm or a pulp plantation, usually on the other side of the world. Every figure is sourced on the slide itself, and where researchers disagree, the post says so instead of picking the most dramatic number.

Plain Water: ___

Every industry hides behind its vocabulary, and water is worse than most. COD, sludge, effluent, ETP: these words decide how water gets treated, priced and regulated, yet almost nobody outside the sector is ever told what they mean. One term per post, explained the way I would explain it to a friend over dinner, with the reason it matters rather than the textbook definition.

Myth: ___

Some of what everyone repeats about water is true, some is half true, and some survives simply because it sounds sensible. This series takes one claim at a time and checks it against the evidence. The honest answer is often that it depends, and what it depends on usually turns out to be the interesting part.

Thirsty Places: ___

Scarcity is easier to understand when it has an address. Singapore built its way out of depending on a neighbour for water. The Aral Sea was drained for cotton and mostly disappeared within a single lifetime. These posts follow what happens when a place runs short and what people did next, and some of the stories end well.

Thirsty Industries: ___

Behind every product is a sector, and sectors use water at a scale that is hard to picture: textiles, food and drink, paper, electronics. This is the series closest to my own work, so it is where the numbers get big and where I can also say which fixes already exist, which ones pay for themselves, and which are still excuses.
