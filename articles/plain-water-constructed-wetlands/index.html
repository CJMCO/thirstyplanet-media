Plain Water: Constructed Wetlands

A bed of gravel planted with reeds can clean the sewage of a village as well as a concrete works does, with no machinery, no chemicals and almost no electricity, if it is given enough land. What a constructed wetland is and what actually does the cleaning in it, the three ways the water can be made to flow and what each is good at, why it needs about five square metres for every person it serves, how France came to build thousands of them, what the fish ponds on the edge of Kolkata have been doing with the city's sewage for a century, and where a reed bed is the wrong answer.

12 November 2026 · By Cesar Chu Ortega, founder of Aguato · 8 min read

Share

Before dawn on the eastern edge of Kolkata, men wade waist deep into shallow ponds that stretch to the horizon and draw nets through water the colour of pea soup. By six the catch, carp and tilapia, is in the city's markets, still moving. The water the fish grew in came out of the city's sewers a few weeks earlier. Nobody designed this. Fishermen found, about a century ago, that sewage let into a shallow pond and left in the tropical sun for some days turns green with algae, that the algae feed the tiny animals that fish eat, and that the water leaving the far end of the pond is clear. The ponds, called bheris, now cover several thousand hectares, and with the vegetable plots and rice fields around them they take in most of the sewage of the core of a city of millions and return fish, food and cleaner water. For most of its history Kolkata had no sewage treatment works for that sewage, because the wetland was doing the job.

Engineers have a name for doing this on purpose. This article is about the constructed wetland: what it is, what actually does the cleaning, how much land it needs and why, where it is the right answer and where it fails.

A bed of gravel

A constructed wetland, in its commonest form, is a shallow basin about as deep as a person's arm is long, lined with plastic or clay so that nothing leaks into the ground, filled with gravel or coarse sand, and planted with reeds. Sewage that has first stood in a tank to let the solids settle is let in at one end and trickles through the bed to the other. It takes several days to cross. What comes out is clear and has lost most of what made it sewage.

The reeds are what a visitor sees, and they do less than the visitor assumes. The cleaning is done by bacteria, the same families of bacteria that work in the aeration tank of a conventional sewage works. The process is in the ETP article on this site. In a works they float in a tank and are kept alive with air blown in by machines. In a wetland they live as a film on the surface of every piece of gravel and every root, and the water is brought slowly past them. The gravel also strains out fine solids, and some substances stick to it.

The plants help in smaller ways. Their roots and underground stems keep channels open through the bed and give the bacteria more surface to live on. They carry a little air down to their roots. Their dead stems lie on the surface in winter as a blanket, so that beds in Canada, Norway and the Czech Republic keep working under snow. And they turn a sewage works into something that looks like a field, which matters to the neighbours. The common reed is the usual choice because it is tough, grows almost anywhere, and survives being flooded with sewage.

Three ways to flow

The idea came from Germany. In the 1950s a botanist named Käthe Seidel, working at a Max Planck institute, grew bulrushes in tanks of polluted water and showed that the water came out cleaner; in the following decades Reinhold Kickuth, a soil scientist at Göttingen, built the first full size beds for villages. From there the method spread to Denmark, Britain and the rest of Europe in the 1980s, and it has settled into three main designs, which differ in how the water moves.

In the first, the water flows over the surface between the plants, as in a natural marsh. This kind is cheap and good for polishing water that has already been treated, and it attracts birds; the town of Arcata in California has run its sewage through such a marsh since the 1980s and the marsh is now a nature reserve. It also needs the most land, and open water breeds mosquitoes.

In the second, the water flows sideways below the surface of the gravel, so there is nothing to see or smell and nowhere for a mosquito to lay. This horizontal bed is the classic reed bed. Because it is always full of water, little air reaches the bacteria, and it is good at removing organic matter and solids and poor at the ammonia that the nitrogen article on this site describes.

In the third, the bed is flooded from the top in batches, several times a day, and drains downward, and each time the water drains, air is drawn into the gravel behind it. With air, the bacteria can deal with ammonia as well, and the bed can be much smaller. The French took this furthest. From the 1990s they built vertical beds in two stages that take raw sewage directly, with no settling tank before them: the solids collect as a layer on top of the first bed, where the reeds grow through them and they slowly turn to compost, to be dug out every ten or fifteen years. More than four thousand French villages are now served this way.

The price is land

Those figures are the trade in one line. A horizontal reed bed needs about five square metres for each person it serves. A conventional sewage works with aeration tanks needs a fraction of a square metre. The wetland gives the bacteria room and time where the works gives them machinery and electricity, and the result is about the same: both remove eighty to ninety five percent of the organic matter and the solids.

For a city the sum does not work. A million people would need five square kilometres of reed bed, on land that costs more than any machine. For a village of three hundred it comes to the size of a few tennis courts at the bottom of a field, and the comparison then runs the other way. A small conventional plant has blowers, pumps and controls that must be watched and repaired by someone trained, and small communities cannot afford that person; their plants break down and stay broken. A reed bed that is fed by gravity has nothing to break. It needs someone to look at it once a week, to check that the inlet is not blocked, and to cut the reeds once a year. Low maintenance does not mean none, and the usual cause of a failed reed bed is that it was forgotten.

There are things a wetland does badly. It removes only part of the nitrogen unless it is built in stages, and very little of the phosphorus, because once the gravel has taken up what it can hold there is nowhere else for it to go; the phosphorus article on this site covers the alternatives. It reduces germs by a factor of ten to a thousand, which is not enough to make the water safe to drink or to swim in. And a horizontal bed slowly clogs with solids at the inlet end, so that after ten to twenty years the gravel there must be dug out and replaced.

Beyond the village

The same principle is used well outside villages. Airports run the water from their runways through reed beds in winter to break down the glycol used to keep ice off aircraft. Mines use them for acid drainage. In the south of Oman, an oilfield that brings up far more salty water than oil sends more than a hundred thousand cubic metres of it a day through reed beds that cover several square kilometres of desert. It is the largest constructed wetland in the world built for industry, and it replaced deep disposal wells that had needed a great deal of power to run.

The ponds of Kolkata

The East Kolkata Wetlands are the oldest and largest example of the idea, and nobody set out to build a treatment plant. They cover about twelve and a half thousand hectares on the eastern side of the city, between it and the old salt marshes of the delta. The city's sewage arrives by canal. In the ponds it is held for some weeks under the sun, and three things happen that no machine is asked to do: algae grow on the nutrients and release oxygen, bacteria use the oxygen to break down the organic matter, and fish eat the small animals that eat the algae. The fishermen manage the ponds by eye and by rule of thumb passed down over three or four generations, letting in sewage in measured amounts and judging by the colour of the water when it is ready for fish.

The system was hardly known outside the fishing cooperatives until the 1980s, when a sanitation engineer of the state government, Dhrubajyoti Ghosh, was sent to find out what happened to the city's sewage and spent the rest of his career explaining what he had found. It was listed as a wetland of international importance in 2002. The threat to it is the obvious one: it is flat open land beside a growing city, and every year some of the ponds are filled and built on, although the law forbids it.

What it teaches

A constructed wetland is a sewage works with the machinery taken out and land put in its place. The biology is the same, and the cleaning is real and measured. What changes is who can run it. A conventional works needs a utility with engineers, power and a budget for spare parts; a reed bed needs a field and someone who remembers it is there. That makes it the right answer for the places conventional treatment never reaches, the village, the farm, the school, the roadside hotel, and the wrong one for a dense city with no land to spare.

Kolkata is the exception, a city that kept its wetland by accident and still gets its fish from it.

Five square metres a person, bacteria on gravel, and reeds that mostly hold the place together.
