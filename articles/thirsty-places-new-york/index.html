Thirsty Places: New York

Nine million people drink water that falls on mountains two hundred kilometres away, runs to the city downhill through tunnels drilled in rock, and for nine tenths of it never passes through a filter. Where New York's water comes from, why a private water company founded by Aaron Burr became a bank, how the city reached into the Catskills and what it flooded there, why it chose to pay farmers and buy forest rather than build a ten billion dollar filtration plant, what is leaking from the longest tunnel in the world, how the city came to use a third less water than it did in 1980, and what a deal between a city and the countryside it drinks from looks like after thirty years.

12 November 2026 · By Cesar Chu Ortega, founder of Aguato · 8 min read

Share

In the Catskill mountains, a hundred and fifty kilometres north of Manhattan, there are dairy farms where the stream at the bottom of the pasture has a new fence along both banks, a strip of young trees inside the fence, and a concrete trough in the field so that the cows drink there and stay out of the water. The farmer did not pay for any of it. The fence, the trees, the trough and a covered yard for the manure beside the barn were designed by a local council of farmers and paid for by the water department of a city the farmer may visit once a year. The stream runs into a reservoir, the reservoir into a tunnel, and some weeks later the water comes out of a tap in Brooklyn, having passed through chlorine and ultraviolet light and no filter of any kind.

This article is about that arrangement: where New York's water comes from, how the city came to reach so far for it, why it decided to look after the land in place of building a filtration plant, what is wearing out underground, and what the deal between the city and the mountains has cost each side.

A company that became a bank

Manhattan is an island of hard rock in a tidal estuary. The rivers on either side of it are salt. For its first two centuries the city drank from wells and from a pond north of the settlement, both of which it also used as drains, and by 1800 the water was foul and yellow fever came most summers. In 1799 the state granted a charter to a private company to bring in clean water. The man behind it, Aaron Burr, had written into the charter a clause allowing the company to use its spare capital for any business it liked. It laid a few kilometres of wooden pipe from a well, and put its money into banking. The bank it became is still trading, under another name, as one of the largest in the world. The water did not improve.

What changed the city's mind was a cholera epidemic in 1832 that killed some three and a half thousand people and a fire in 1835 that burned most of the business district while the firemen's hoses froze. Voters approved a public supply by a wide margin. Engineers dammed the Croton river, sixty five kilometres to the north, and built a masonry aqueduct to carry its water to the city by gravity alone, on a slope of about twenty centimetres to the kilometre. It opened in 1842, and the pattern was set: public ownership, a distant clean source, and gravity.

Into the mountains

The city outgrew the Croton within fifty years and went further. Between 1907 and 1928 it built two large reservoirs in the Catskill mountains and an aqueduct of a hundred and fifty kilometres that passes under the Hudson river through solid rock, more than three hundred metres below the surface. Between 1937 and 1964 it went further again, to the headwaters of the Delaware river on the far side of the mountains, and built four more reservoirs and a tunnel of a hundred and thirty seven kilometres, which is still the longest continuous tunnel in the world.

The reservoirs filled valleys where people lived. More than twenty villages and hamlets were taken by compulsory purchase and cleared, their houses burned or moved, their graveyards dug up and relocated, and several thousand people were sent to find homes elsewhere, for the benefit of a city most of them had never seen. That history is remembered in the mountain towns, and it is the background to everything that came later.

The result is a system of unusual simplicity. The watershed covers about five thousand square kilometres, most of it forest. The water moves from reservoir to reservoir and settles as it goes, then enters the city through three tunnels in the bedrock, the newest of which was begun in 1970 and is still being finished. Almost none of it is pumped, because the reservoirs stand a hundred metres and more above the city. The pressure that arrives is enough to reach about the sixth floor of a building, which is the reason for the wooden tanks on the roofs that the water towers article on this site explains.

The filter that was not built

In 1989 a new federal rule required every American city that drinks from rivers or lakes to filter its water, unless it could prove that the source was clean enough and well enough protected to go without. Only a handful of large cities qualified, among them Boston, San Francisco, Seattle and Portland. New York applied for the same exemption for its Catskill and Delaware water. The alternative was a filtration plant for a billion gallons a day, the largest ever built, then estimated at six to ten billion dollars with several hundred million a year to run it.

The federal agency was willing, on one condition: the city had to control what happened on the land around its reservoirs. The city owned very little of that land, and its first move, a set of draft rules for the mountain towns written without them, brought a lawsuit from a coalition of those towns and nearly ended the attempt. What came out of two years of negotiation, in January 1997, was a document signed by the city, the state, the federal agency, about seventy upstate towns and villages and a group of environmental organisations.

Its terms have governed the watershed ever since. The city buys land only from owners who want to sell, pays the market price, and pays local taxes on what it buys. It pays to repair or replace failing household septic systems, the subject of the septic tanks article on this site, several thousand of them so far. It paid to rebuild the villages' sewage works to a standard far above what the villages could have afforded. It funds the farmers' council that draws up a plan for each farm and builds what the plan calls for, at no cost to the farmer. And it gave the towns a fund for their own economic development, in return for accepting limits on building near the water.

A panel of the national academies of science reviewed the programme in 2020 and found that it had worked: the water continues to meet the standard, at a fraction of the cost of the plant. The exemption is not a permanent gift. The older Croton system, whose watershed filled with suburbs during the twentieth century, could not meet the standard, and the city was ordered to filter it; the plant, built under a golf course in the Bronx, opened in 2015 and cost more than three billion dollars for a tenth of the supply. And the mountain water does get one modern treatment: since 2013 all of it passes through the largest ultraviolet plant in the world, whose lamps kill the chlorine resistant parasites that the disinfection article on this site describes.

The leak

The weakness of a system built on a few great tunnels is that the tunnels cannot easily be turned off. The Delaware aqueduct, which carries about half the city's water, has been leaking where it passes through weak limestone near the Hudson since at least the early 1990s, at a rate the city puts at twenty to thirty five million gallons a day, something like a hundred million litres. Houses above the leak have water in their basements.

A tunnel that carries half the supply cannot be drained for inspection until there is another way to bring the water in. The city spent a decade preparing: it drove a bypass tunnel four kilometres long under the river, two hundred metres down, and brought its other sources up to full capacity. In the autumn of 2024 it shut the aqueduct to connect the bypass. Within weeks an unusually dry autumn set in, the reservoirs still in service fell, the city declared its first drought warning in more than twenty years, and the aqueduct was turned back on with the work unfinished and the shutdown put off.

A third less

The quietest achievement is at the other end of the pipe. Around 1980 New York used about one and a half billion gallons of water a day and was planning for more. Most homes paid a flat charge based on the width of the building, and nobody had a reason to mend a running toilet. From the late 1980s the city fitted a meter to every property and billed by volume, and in the 1990s it paid owners to replace more than a million old toilets with ones that use a third of the water. Consumption fell to about a billion gallons a day and has stayed there, while the population rose by more than a million. The new sources that were being planned in the 1970s were never needed.

What it teaches

New York made a choice that most cities on this site never had: it could keep its water clean at the source because the source was forest and small farms, and because it started early. The lesson travels further than the geography. The city found that the cheapest treatment plant was the land itself, and that the land could be protected only with the consent of the people living on it, who had every reason to distrust the city. The agreement of 1997 pays those people to do what the city needs, and both sides have kept it for nearly thirty years.

It does not run itself. The climate is bringing heavier storms that wash clay into the reservoirs, the tunnels are a century old, and one of them still leaks.

Nine tenths unfiltered, a fence along a stream in the Catskills, and a tunnel that cannot yet be switched off.
