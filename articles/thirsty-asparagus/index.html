Thirsty: Asparagus

The asparagus on a European or American plate in December was cut two days earlier in a desert on the coast of Peru where it almost never rains, and every litre it drank came out of an aquifer that is falling faster than almost any other on Earth. Where a spear's water goes, why Peru grows it all year in a valley with no rain, what forty years of export farming have done to the water under the Ica valley and to the people who share it, why the same crop two hundred kilometres north drinks a river instead, and what a bunch bought in spring from a field nearby has in common with one flown in from Ica, which is very little.

12 November 2026 · By Cesar Chu Ortega, founder of Aguato · 8 min read

Share

A few kilometres west of the city of Ica, on the coast of Peru, there is a lagoon in a bowl of sand dunes with palm trees around it and a ring of small hotels, and it appears on the back of the fifty sol banknote. Huacachina is a real oasis, one of the very few in the Americas: for as long as anyone recorded it, groundwater seeped up through the sand and kept the lagoon full. In the 1980s the level began to drop. Today the water in it is put there by a pump, paid for by the people whose living depends on visitors believing in it, and the reason is in the fields on the other side of the dunes, where the same groundwater is drawn up through a couple of thousand wells and turned into asparagus.

A kilogram of asparagus carries about 2,150 litres of water on the world's average, and this article is about why that average tells you almost nothing about the bunch in your hand, why a desert became one of the largest asparagus farms on Earth, what that has done to the water under it, and what the same vegetable looks like when it grows where it rains.

A shoot that grows in a day

Asparagus is the young shoot of a plant that would, if left alone, grow into a feathery bush as tall as a person. The spear is cut when it is about twenty centimetres out of the ground, and in warm soil a spear can grow that much in a day or two, so a field is walked and cut every morning for weeks. What sits under the field is a crown of roots that lives for ten or fifteen years and stores, in the months when the bush is left standing, the sugar that next season's spears are made from. The plant therefore drinks all year for a harvest of a few weeks, and it drinks deeply, because the fern is a large surface of green in the sun.

On the Water Footprint Network's method, which this site uses for every crop, the global average comes to about 2,150 litres for a kilogram. That is high for a vegetable. A kilogram of tomatoes carries about 214 litres and a kilogram of potatoes about 290, and the reason asparagus stands so far above them is the yield: a hectare gives five to ten tonnes of spears where it would give forty of potatoes, so the same season's water is divided among far fewer kilograms.

Seven tenths of that average is rain, and the irrigation share is small. The average is made of fields in China, which grows most of the world's asparagus, in Germany, in Mexico and in Peru, and it hides the one fact that matters about any single bunch, which is where the field was.

A desert with no winter

The coast of Peru is one of the driest places on Earth. The cold Humboldt current offshore cools the air so that it cannot rise and make rain, which is the mechanism the Lima article on this site describes, and the Ica valley, three hundred kilometres south of the capital, receives a few millimetres in a normal year. It also has no frost, sunshine most days and sandy soil, and in the 1980s growers there found that asparagus, a plant that elsewhere sleeps through the winter and gives one harvest in spring, could be managed by water alone. Stop the irrigation and the plant goes dormant, as if winter had come; start it again and the spears come up. A field in Ica can be cut twice a year, in whichever months the grower chooses, and the yields are among the highest in the world.

The months the growers chose were the ones when Europe and North America have no asparagus of their own. An American trade law of 1991, meant to give Andean farmers something to grow other than coca, let Peruvian vegetables into the United States free of duty, and within twenty years Peru was the largest exporter of fresh asparagus in the world, a position it now trades with Mexico from year to year. The spears are cut in the morning, cooled within hours, and flown out of Lima the same night. The valley that had grown cotton and grapes for local markets on river water became a strip of drip irrigated fields a hundred square kilometres wide, feeding supermarkets nine thousand kilometres away, and the jobs it created, tens of thousands of them, are the reason the story is harder than it first looks.

What the wells did

The Ica river runs for a few months a year, when it rains in the Andes, and the old farms of the valley floor used it. The export farms were laid out on the desert beside the valley, on land that had never been farmed because it had no water, and they drilled. Drip irrigation is efficient, in the sense that nearly every litre reaches a root, and an asparagus field under drip in Ica still takes about fifteen thousand cubic metres of water a hectare a year, because the sun is fierce and the plant is kept green for most of the year. With the yields reported there, that is something over a thousand litres of pumped groundwater for each kilogram cut, by this site's arithmetic, and almost no rain at all. The world average has the proportions the other way round.

A study published in 2010 by a group of British and Peruvian organisations, which remains the fullest account of the valley, set the national water authority's own figures side by side: the farms were taking out of the aquifer roughly twice what the river and the mountains put back in. The water table was falling by half a metre to two metres a year across the valley and by as much as eight metres a year in the worst places, which the authors described as among the fastest falls of any aquifer in the world.

A falling water table does not reach everyone at the same time. An export company can deepen its wells to a hundred metres and beyond, and pay for the electricity to lift the water. A small farmer on the valley floor with a hand dug well of fifteen metres cannot, and neither can a district of the city whose municipal well was drilled in the 1970s. The study found small farmers selling their land to the larger farms because their wells had failed, and neighbourhoods of Ica, a city of several hundred thousand people, receiving water for a few hours a day. The government banned new wells in the valley, and wells went on being drilled. Some of the large growers now bring water from outside the valley by pipeline and canal, which moves the question to the next valley.

The same crop on a river

Two hundred kilometres north of Lima, in the region of La Libertad, the other half of Peru's asparagus grows on a different water. A state irrigation scheme begun in the 1980s takes water from the Santa river, which is fed by the glaciers and the rains of the Cordillera Blanca, and carries it by canal along the coast to three desert valleys. Much of the asparagus there is the white kind, grown under mounds of sand and sold in jars and tins, and it drinks a river that still reaches the sea. That scheme has its own arguments, about who received the new land and about what happens as the glaciers shrink, and it is still a renewable supply in a way that the Ica aquifer has stopped being.

So two bunches from the same country, with the same flag on the label, can stand on entirely different water. That is the general point of the groundwater article on this site: the footprint tells you how much, and only the place tells you whether it will be there next year.

The spring crop

The asparagus that Europe waited for before the aeroplane was a spring vegetable. In Germany, which eats more of it per person than any other country, the season opens in April and ends by custom on the twenty fourth of June, so that the plants have the rest of the summer to grow their fern and refill their roots. The fields of Lower Saxony, Brandenburg and the upper Rhine are sandy and rain fed for most of the year, with some irrigation in a dry spring, and their footprint looks like the world average in the table above: mostly green water, falling where it falls whether or not anyone plants asparagus under it. The same is true of the fields of the English Vale of Evesham, of Michigan, and of most of the Chinese crop.

That asparagus is available for ten weeks. The Peruvian trade exists to fill the other forty two, and it has made a seasonal luxury into something that sits in the chilled aisle all year at a steady price. The price does not include the well in Ica that went dry.

What it teaches

Asparagus is the clearest case on this site of a number that needs an address. The global figure is high and mostly harmless, rain on sandy fields in cool countries, a few weeks a year. The Ica figure is of the same size and made of something else: water that fell on the Andes centuries ago, lifted from deeper every year, in a valley where a city and several thousand small farmers have a claim on it too, and where the lagoon on the banknote is kept full by a pump. The trade also feeds tens of thousands of families in a poor region, and the people who have studied the valley most closely ask for the wells to be measured and limited, and do not ask for the farms to close.

For the person standing at the shelf, the useful facts are the two on the label: the country and the month. A bunch from nearby in spring and a bunch from a desert in December are the same vegetable and very different water.

About 2,150 litres a kilogram on average, most of it rain, and in one valley in Peru almost none of it.
