Thirsty

The water in the things we buy: one number for each product, and where it was spent.

Everything we buy was made with water, and almost all of it was used before the thing reached us. A cup of coffee holds about 125 millilitres and took around 140 litres to grow, wash, roast and brew. A cotton t-shirt took 2,700. A kilogram of beef, more than 15,000. These articles put one number on one product and then explain it: where the water was used, whether it was rain or a river or a well, and what was left behind.

The method is the water footprint, developed by researchers in the Netherlands and used here throughout. It counts three kinds of water: green, the rain a crop drinks from the soil; blue, the water pumped from rivers and aquifers; and grey, the volume needed to dilute what runs off. The split matters more than the total. Rain on a pasture in Ireland and groundwater under an almond orchard in a drought are both litres, and they are very different litres.

A large footprint made of rain in a wet country is a smaller burden than a modest one pumped from a falling aquifer, and the point of the series is to make that distinction second nature.
