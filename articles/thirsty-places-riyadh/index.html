Thirsty Places: Riyadh

A city of more than seven million people stands six hundred metres up on a desert plateau, four hundred kilometres from the sea, and drinks the Gulf. Where Riyadh's water comes from, how seawater is made fresh on the coast and pumped uphill for the length of a small country, what lies in the deep hot aquifers under the city and how much of it was spent growing wheat for export in the 1980s, why the kingdom stopped, why the people of the driest large country on Earth use more water each than almost anyone, and what a capital planning to double in size intends to drink.

12 November 2026 · By Cesar Chu Ortega, founder of Aguato · 7 min read

Share

Seen from orbit in the 1990s, the desert of central and northern Saudi Arabia was covered in green dots. Each one was a perfect circle about a kilometre across, and there were thousands of them, laid out in rows across land where it rains a few days a year. Each circle was a field of wheat, watered by a long arm of pipe on wheels that turned slowly around a well at the centre. The well went down several hundred metres to water that had been in the rock since before farming was invented. For a few years in the early 1990s the kingdom, with no river and no lake, was one of the half dozen largest exporters of wheat in the world.

The circles are mostly brown now, and the capital that ordered them is one of the fastest growing cities on Earth. This article is about how Riyadh gets its water: the sea, pumped uphill for the length of a small country; the deep hot water under the city; what the wheat cost; and what a city planning to double its population intends to drink.

The gardens

The name means the gardens. Riyadh began as a group of walled farming villages along Wadi Hanifah, a dry valley that runs for more than a hundred kilometres through the plateau of Najd and carries a flood a few times a year. Rain soaked into the gravel of the valley floor, shallow wells brought it up, and date palms grew in a strip a few hundred metres wide. In 1930 the town had perhaps thirty thousand people, and the valley was enough for them.

Oil money arrived after the Second World War and the town became a capital. By 1970 it had half a million people and by 1990 two million, and the shallow water of the wadi had been pumped out long before. The city is now home to more than seven million, on a plateau six hundred metres above the sea, with about a hundred millimetres of rain in a year and summer days of forty five degrees.

The sea, uphill

The first answer was under the city. In the 1950s drillers reached an aquifer called the Minjur at more than a thousand metres, and later the Wasia, further east. Both hold very old water, and both are deep enough that the water comes up at fifty to eighty degrees and carries too much dissolved salt to drink as it is. It has to be cooled in towers and passed through membranes before it reaches a tap. The method is in the reverse osmosis article on this site. The deep wells still supply a large part of the city.

The second answer was the Gulf. In 1983 a desalination plant at Jubail, on the coast, began sending water to the capital through a pair of steel pipelines about 470 kilometres long, with pumping stations along the way to lift it nearly seven hundred metres. It was among the largest water transfers ever built, and it has been doubled since: a second plant at Ras Al Khair, further up the coast, opened in 2014 as the largest of its kind in the world, making about a million cubic metres a day, most of it for Riyadh. More than half of the city's water now begins as seawater.

The desalination article on this site sets out what that costs. The older plants boiled seawater with heat from burning oil and gas, and the pumping inland takes more energy again, so for decades the kingdom burned a noticeable share of its own oil to make its capital's water. The newer plants use membranes, which need about a third of the energy, and some of the newest draw part of their power from solar fields. Saudi Arabia makes roughly a fifth of all the desalinated water in the world.

A city at the end of two pipelines is exposed in a particular way. For most of its modern history Riyadh held only a day or two of water in storage, so that a fault at a plant or a break in a line would have reached the taps within the week. The national water strategy published in 2018 set a target of a week's reserve for the cities, and large covered reservoirs are being built on the edge of the capital.

The wheat

The deep aquifers of the Arabian peninsula filled during wet periods that ended several thousand years ago, when the peninsula had lakes and grassland. Almost nothing reaches them today. They are the kind of store the groundwater article on this site calls fossil water: a quantity, which can be used once.

In the late 1970s the kingdom decided to grow its own food. The memory of the oil embargo of 1973, when Arab producers had cut off the West and American officials had talked openly of cutting off grain in return, made dependence on imported wheat look dangerous. The government gave away desert land, lent the money for wells and pivots without interest, sold diesel for almost nothing, and bought the wheat at several times the world price. Production rose from almost none to about four million tonnes in 1992, twice what the country could eat. The surplus was exported, and some of it was given away.

An economist who later added up the programme estimated that farming in the kingdom used about three hundred billion cubic metres of water between 1980 and 1999, roughly two thirds of it from aquifers that will not refill, at a cost to the state of tens of billions of dollars. A tonne of wheat grown that way took about two thousand cubic metres of water that could have supplied a household in Riyadh for several years. Water tables under the farming regions fell by tens of metres and in places by more than a hundred.

The kingdom reversed itself in stages. Wheat exports were wound down in the 1990s. In 2008 the government announced that it would stop buying home grown wheat altogether, and the last purchase was in 2016. The farmers switched to alfalfa for the dairy herds, which drinks even more than wheat, and the government then restricted that too. The large dairy companies now grow or buy their fodder abroad, and one of them bought farmland in Arizona to grow alfalfa on groundwater there and ship it home, an arrangement the Phoenix article on this site touches on and which the state of Arizona moved to end in 2023. The water saved in Arabia was, in part, moved to someone else's aquifer.

Agriculture still takes most of the water in the kingdom, largely for palms and fodder. That is the subject of the dates article on this site.

The cheapest water in the world

People in Riyadh use about 260 litres of water each a day at home, by the government's own figure. That is about twice what a Londoner uses and among the highest rates anywhere. Part of the reason is the climate, and gardens that must be watered every day of the year. Most of it was the price. For decades water was sold at a few cents a cubic metre, a small fraction of what it cost to make and pump, and a household had no reason to notice how much it used.

In 2016, with the oil price low, the government raised the tariff sharply and without much warning. Bills arrived that were ten times the previous ones, the public anger was loud enough that the minister responsible was dismissed within months, and the increase was softened. The price is still below cost. The strategy of 2018 aims to bring household use down to 150 litres a person by 2030, through meters, pricing and repairs to a network that loses something like a quarter of what is put into it.

There is one more supply, and it is the one the city makes itself. Riyadh's treated sewage is used to water its parks and roadside trees, and part of it is released into Wadi Hanifah, which had become an open drain and a rubbish tip by the 1990s. A restoration project cleared the valley, built a chain of pools and planted beds where the water is cleaned further by the same natural processes the constructed wetlands article on this site describes, and the old dry valley now has a stream in it all year, fed by the city's own used water. It won an international architecture award in 2010.

Doubling

The government's plan is for Riyadh to grow to something like fifteen million people in the 2030s, with new districts, a new airport and a world exhibition in 2030. Every additional person will be supplied from the sea. New membrane plants are being built on the Gulf coast and new pipelines laid beside the old ones, and the deep aquifers are to be kept as a reserve for emergencies, which is what a store that cannot refill is best used for.

What it teaches

Riyadh is the city on this site that is furthest from its water in every sense. It has no river, no usable rain and no aquifer that refills, and it has grown to seven million people on energy alone: energy to make the sea fresh and energy to push it uphill. That works for as long as the energy is cheap and the pipelines hold. The harder lesson is the wheat. A country with almost no water spent a large part of what it had, in twenty years, on a crop it could have bought, and then had the courage to stop. Most places that mine their groundwater have not yet managed the second part.

Four hundred and seventy kilometres of pipe, a lift of seven hundred metres, and circles in the desert that have gone brown.
