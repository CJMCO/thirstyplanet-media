Plain Water: Water Towers

The tank on legs at the edge of a town is the reason water comes out of the tap when it is opened, at the same force on the top floor as on the ground, at seven in the morning when everyone showers at once and at three at night when the pumps are off. What a water tower is for, why its height matters and its size matters less, how it lets a small pump serve a town, why a hill does the same job for free, what the wooden tanks on the roofs of New York are doing there, how Kuwait turned its towers into a national monument, and why a tower that is too full for too long becomes a problem of its own.

12 November 2026 · By Cesar Chu Ortega, founder of Aguato · 8 min read

Share

Across the middle of the United States, where the land is flat for a thousand kilometres, the first thing a traveller sees of any town is its water tower. It comes over the horizon before the grain elevator and the church: a white steel ball on a single stem, or an older tank on four legs with a pointed roof, with the name of the town painted round it in letters two metres high and sometimes the name of the high school team. Nobody thinks about it much. It is a landmark, a place to hang the mobile phone antennas, a thing teenagers climb. It is also the reason that when anyone in the town opens a tap, water comes out with force, on the ground floor and the top floor alike, whether or not a single pump in the town is running at that moment.

This article is about what the tower does and how, why a hill does the same job for free, what the wooden tanks on the roofs of New York are for, how Kuwait made monuments of its towers, and what goes wrong inside one.

Height is pressure

Water in a pipe is pushed by the weight of the water above it. Stand a column of water ten metres tall and the pressure at the bottom is very nearly one bar, the same again as the air around us, and it does not matter whether the column is as wide as a lake or as narrow as a straw. Only the height counts.

A household tap works well at somewhere between two and five bar. Below about one and a half, a shower on an upper floor becomes a trickle and a boiler may refuse to light; much above six, pipes knock, fittings leak and a burst main loses a great deal of water quickly. So a water system has to hold every tap in the town inside that band, and the simplest way ever found to do it is to keep a body of water thirty to fifty metres above the taps and connect it to the pipes. The pressure then comes from gravity. It needs no moving parts and no electricity, and it does not vary, except by the metre or two that the level in the tank rises and falls in a day.

A battery for the morning

The tower is connected to the network by a single pipe, and water flows in and out of it through that pipe according to who is winning, the pumps or the town. This is the second thing it does, and the engineers call it equalising.

A town does not use water evenly. Between six and eight in the morning, when people shower, flush and fill kettles within the same hour, demand runs at two or three times the day's average; there is a second, lower peak in the evening; and between midnight and five almost nothing is used at all. Without storage, the treatment works and the pumps would have to be big enough for the morning peak and would stand mostly idle for the other twenty two hours. With a tower, they can be sized for the average and run at a steady rate. At night, when the town is asleep, the pumps deliver more than is being used and the surplus rises into the tank. In the morning the town draws more than the pumps deliver and the tank makes up the difference. The level goes down a few metres by nine o'clock and is back by dawn the next day. In many places the pumping is done at night on purpose, when electricity is cheapest.

The same stored water does two more jobs. Part of it is a reserve for fire: a fire engine at a hydrant can draw more water in a few minutes than a neighbourhood uses in an hour, and the tower supplies that surge without the pressure collapsing for everyone else. And part is a reserve for failure. If the power goes off or a pump breaks, the tower keeps the town supplied for some hours, typically the better part of a day, which is usually long enough to start a generator.

That reserve matters for a reason that is easy to miss. The leaks article on this site explains that water pipes are never perfectly tight, and that what keeps the dirty water in the ground out of the clean water in the pipe is the pressure inside. A network that loses pressure, even for an hour, can draw contaminated groundwater in through the same cracks that normally leak outward. This is why a utility issues a notice to boil water after a large burst or a power failure, and why keeping the tower from running empty is the first rule of the control room.

The hill

A tower is what a town builds when it has no hill. Where there is high ground within reach, the same job is done by a covered tank dug into the top of it, which the British call a service reservoir. It is cheaper to build, it can be far larger, and it needs no legs. Most hilly cities are supplied this way, and their residents never see the tanks at all, which sit under grass mounds on the heights with a small brick building beside them. The water tower is therefore a mark of flat country: the American plains, the Netherlands, northern Germany, the Paris basin, the Gulf.

The earliest ones were not really tanks. When steam pumps came into use in the nineteenth century they pushed water in pulses, and a tall open pipe was stood beside the pumping station to absorb the shocks, with its top above the highest pressure the pump could give. These standpipes were dressed in stone and brick to look like towers and columns. The one at Louisville in Kentucky, finished in 1860, is the oldest still standing in America, and the one in Chicago, of 1869, is one of the few buildings in the centre of the city that survived the great fire two years later.

The roofs of New York

New York's water comes from reservoirs in the mountains to the north and arrives in the city by gravity, with enough pressure in the mains to push it up to about the sixth floor of a building. In the 1880s, when buildings began to rise above that, the city required each tall building to lift its own water, and the answer that owners settled on has not changed since. A pump in the basement sends water to a tank on the roof, and gravity brings it down again to every floor below. Each tall building is a small town with its own tower.

There are more than ten thousand of these tanks in the city; estimates run from twelve to seventeen thousand. Most are still built of wood, cedar or redwood, by two or three family firms that have been doing it for over a century. The staves are cut to fit and held by steel hoops with no glue or sealant, and when the tank is first filled it leaks from every seam for a day, until the wood swells and closes. Wood is light, it is cheap, it can be carried up in a lift and assembled on the roof in a day, and it insulates well enough that the water does not freeze in winter. A tank holds twenty to forty thousand litres and lasts about thirty years. The pipe that feeds the building's taps is set part of the way up the side, so that the water below it can never be drawn off by ordinary use and is always there for the fire sprinklers.

The weakness is upkeep. City law requires each tank to be inspected and cleaned every year, and a newspaper investigation in 2014 found that many owners did not do it, and that samples from the bottom of some tanks contained the bacteria the E. coli article on this site describes. Enforcement has tightened since. The New York article on this site covers the reservoirs at the other end of the pipe.

Kuwait's mushrooms

Kuwait has no river and almost no rain, and since the 1950s nearly all of its fresh water has been made from the sea. Distilled water is produced at a steady rate, day and night, and a city uses it in peaks, so storage was part of the system from the beginning. In the 1960s the government commissioned a Swedish firm to design it, and what was finished in the 1970s is the most admired set of water towers in the world: thirty three of them, in six groups around the city. Most are concrete mushrooms, a slender stem opening into a wide flat cap, painted in stripes and standing together like a grove, each holding about three thousand cubic metres. The last group, on a point of land in the bay, is the Kuwait Towers, three spikes of which two carry spheres covered in blue, green and grey steel discs. Together the system stores about a hundred thousand cubic metres. The towers won one of the first Aga Khan awards for architecture in 1980, and they are on the country's banknotes.

Old water

A tower has one fault, and it comes from the same stillness that makes it useful. Water that sits in a tank for days loses the small dose of chlorine that the disinfection article on this site describes, the guard that protects it on the way to the tap. In summer the water in a steel tank warms and settles into layers, with the newest water going in and out at the bottom and the oldest sitting at the top for weeks. In a hard winter ice forms on the surface and can tear the inside of the tank as the level moves.

The cure is to keep the water moving. Operators let the level fall well down every day, so that a large share of the tank is exchanged, and newer tanks have mixers or inlet nozzles that stir the contents. A tank that is too large for its town is a common cause of stale water at the far end of a network, which is one reason that a bigger tower is not always a better one.

What it teaches

Some utilities now do without towers, using pumps whose speed follows the demand minute by minute, and in parts of Europe old towers have been emptied and sold as houses. The pumps work. They also stop when the power does, and a tower does not. It is one of the few parts of a water system with no moving parts, it has done the same job since the 1860s, and the principle fits in a sentence: lift the water once, at night, and let gravity deliver it in the morning.

Ten metres of height for each bar, a tank that breathes once a day, and a wooden barrel on a roof in Manhattan doing what the steel ball does on the plains.
