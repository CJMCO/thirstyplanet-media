Thirsty Industries: Soft Drinks

A bottling plant takes less than two litres of water to make a litre of soft drink, and that small, well managed number has started some of the fiercest fights over water anywhere, because the plant takes its water from one place, every day, beside people who drink from the same ground. Where a soft drink's water goes inside the plant and far outside it, what happened at Plachimada in Kerala, where a village closed a Coca Cola factory, why a town in the highlands of Mexico drinks more cola than water, what the companies mean when they say they give back every litre, and why the sugar in the bottle carries fifty times the water of the plant.

12 November 2026 · By Cesar Chu Ortega, founder of Aguato · 8 min read

Share

Plachimada is a village of rice fields and coconut palms in the Palakkad district of Kerala, in the south of India, a few kilometres from the border with Tamil Nadu. In March 2000 a bottling plant opened on a plot of about fourteen hectares at its edge, built by the Indian subsidiary of the Coca Cola Company, with a licence from the village council and six wells of its own. Within two years the people who lived along its wall, most of them from tribal and landless families, were saying the same things to anyone who would listen. The water in their wells had dropped. What was left had turned hard and salty and gave the children stomach pains, and rice cooked in it went bad by the afternoon. In April 2002 a group of women sat down in a shelter of palm leaves across the road from the factory gate and stayed there, in shifts, for years.

A bottling plant uses less than two litres of water to make a litre of drink, and this article is about why so small a number causes such large trouble: where the water goes in the plant, what happened at Plachimada and in a town in Mexico, what the companies mean when they say they return every litre, and where most of a soft drink's water really is.

Inside the plant

A soft drink is water, sugar or a sweetener, carbon dioxide, and a small amount of flavouring concentrate that arrives in drums. The plant's first job is to make the water identical to the water in every other plant of the brand, so the incoming supply, from a town main or the plant's own wells, is filtered, often passed through reverse osmosis, and disinfected, whatever its quality on arrival. Then it is mixed, chilled, carbonated and filled, at speeds of a thousand bottles a minute and more on the fast lines.

The industry's measure is the water use ratio: litres of water taken in for each litre of drink sent out. One litre is the product. The rest goes on rinsing bottles and cans, on cleaning tanks and pipes between flavours with hot water and caustic, which the food article on this site describes, on cooling, on the boilers, and on the reject stream of the plant's own treatment. The Coca Cola Company, which publishes the figure for its whole system, reported 2.7 litres per litre in 2004 and about 1.8 in the early 2020s. Its competitors report much the same.

There is one awkward detail in the table. A returnable glass bottle, which is the better answer to plastic waste, has to be washed in hot caustic and rinsed before it is filled again, and a line of returnable glass uses noticeably more water per litre than a line of new plastic bottles that are simply blown clean with air. What leaves the plant as waste is water with sugar in it, strong enough to strip the oxygen from a stream. That is the measure the COD article on this site explains, and the large companies require their bottlers to treat it to a standard that will support fish, whatever the local law says.

So the plant is efficient, and it is getting more so. The trouble is in the other property of a bottling plant, which is that it draws all its water from one place.

Plachimada

By its own account the plant at Plachimada pumped about half a million litres of groundwater a day; the villagers and some officials put the figure at up to three times that. Around it lay a farming district in the rain shadow of the Western Ghats, drier than the rest of Kerala, where two poor monsoons followed the opening. The company's position, then and since, was that the falling wells were the drought. The villagers' position was that the drought had not turned the water salty.

Two things turned a local protest into a national case. In 2003 a British radio programme had the sludge that the plant was giving to farmers as fertiliser tested, and a laboratory found cadmium and lead in it; the state's pollution board confirmed the finding and ordered the practice stopped. In the same year the village council, the panchayat, refused to renew the plant's licence, on the ground that it was taking the water its people depended on. The plant stopped production in March 2004 and never restarted.

The courts took years over the question underneath, which is who owns the water under a piece of land. A single judge of the Kerala High Court held in 2003 that groundwater is a public resource held in trust by the state, and that a landowner may take only what a farm of that size would. Two judges of the same court reversed him in 2005 and allowed the company half a million litres a day. The appeal sat in the Supreme Court until 2017, when the company told the court it did not intend to reopen the plant.

In 2010 a committee appointed by the state government assessed the damage to the village's water, farms and health at a little over 216 crore rupees, about 48 million dollars at the time, and recommended a tribunal to make the company pay. The state assembly passed the law to create one. It needed the assent of the President of India, which never came. Nothing has been paid.

Plachimada was the first of several. An assessment the company itself commissioned from an Indian research institute in 2008 looked at its plant at Kaladera, in Rajasthan, where the water table had been falling for years before the factory arrived, and recommended that the company find water from outside the area, move the plant or close it. Kaladera closed in 2016.

The town that drinks cola

San Cristóbal de las Casas stands at 2,200 metres in the highlands of Chiapas, in the south of Mexico, in one of the wettest regions of the country. Many of its neighbourhoods have running water a few days a week, and what arrives is often not safe to drink, so families buy water from tankers and in jugs. On the edge of the town is a bottling plant owned by the largest Coca Cola bottler in the world, with federal permits to draw more than a million litres a day from deep wells, for which it pays the federal government a small fee and the town nothing.

A cola in the town's shops costs about the same as a bottle of water and is easier to find. Researchers in Chiapas have estimated that people in the highlands drink more than two litres of soft drinks a day on average, which would be the highest figure in the world, and diabetes has become one of the leading causes of death in the state. In some of the indigenous communities around the town, cola has taken a place in religious ceremonies that used to belong to other drinks.

The plant did not cause the town's water shortage, which comes from leaking pipes, deforested hills and a municipal utility without money, and its wells are far deeper than anyone else's. That is the company's answer, and it is accurate as far as it goes. The picture is still the same as at Plachimada: a factory with a secure supply of good water beside households that have neither, selling them the substitute.

Giving back every litre

In 2007 the Coca Cola Company promised to return to nature and to communities, by 2020, an amount of water equal to what it used in its finished drinks. It declared the target met in 2015. The water is returned through projects, several hundred of them: replanting watersheds, building rainwater tanks for schools, fixing village supplies, and paying for irrigation that wastes less.

The projects are real and some are good. The claim has two limits that are worth knowing. It counts the litre in the bottle and leaves out the water that grew the ingredients, which the next section shows is nearly all of it. And water is local, as every story on this site says in some way: a wetland restored in one river basin does nothing for a well beside a plant in another. The company's newer strategy, for 2030, accepts the second point and promises to concentrate on the plants in places where water is under stress.

The sugar

A half litre bottle of cola holds about fifty grams of sugar. The figures are in the sugar article on this site, which puts cane sugar at about 1,782 litres a kilogram and beet sugar at about 920 on the Water Footprint Network's method, so the sugar alone carries around ninety litres if it came from cane and around forty five if it came from beet. The plant, at 1.8 litres per litre, accounts for less than a litre of the bottle's water.

Two studies have done the whole sum and come out in different places. The company and a conservation group worked through a half litre bottle made in the Netherlands with Dutch beet sugar and arrived at about 35 litres. Researchers at the University of Twente, counting more of the minor ingredients and allowing for cane and for maize syrup from drier places, arrived at 169 to 309 litres. Both agree on the shape: the factory is under one percent, and the fields are the rest.

What it teaches

The soft drink industry has done what this site asks of an industry. It measures its water, publishes the number, and has cut it by a third in twenty years. It has also shown the limit of a number. A water use ratio is an average over hundreds of plants, and a well is in one village. The plant at Plachimada would have looked efficient on any chart, and the question the women at its gate were asking was a different one: whose water it was.

For the person holding the bottle, the larger part of its water is the sugar, and the simplest way to use less of it is the one the doctors already recommend.

About 1.8 litres at the plant for each litre in the bottle, a hundred more in the cane field, and a village in Kerala still waiting to be paid.
