Thirsty Places

Cities and river basins that ran short, and what they did about it.

Some places have already lived through what the rest of the world is being warned about. Cape Town counted down to Day Zero. Chennai ran its water in by train. Mexico City and Jakarta are sinking because they drink the ground beneath them. Singapore, Windhoek and Orange County drink their own sewage, and are proud of it. These articles go to those places.

Each one asks the same questions. Where did the city's water come from, and what changed? What did the shortage or the flood actually look like on the street? What was built, decided or banned afterwards, and did it work? The answers repeat across continents: measure what leaves the system, fix the pipes, price the water, reuse what has been used, catch the rain, and tell the public the truth in time to help.

The series is arranged by place rather than by problem, because the problem is nearly always the same and the place is what makes it real.

7 min Thirsty Places: Bangalore India's technology capital was built on a plateau of lakes, nine hundred metres above the sea and a hundred kilometres from a river, and it drained the lakes, built over them, and pumped the ground beneath until, in the summer of 2024, the wells of half the city ran dry and the technology companies told their staff to work from home. What Bangalore's water is, where the lakes went, what a lake fire looks like, and what the city is trying to get back. 22 September 2026 7 min Thirsty Places: Cairo A country of a hundred and ten million people, almost all of them living on a strip of river valley that is three percent of its land, drinking a river that starts in another country, which has just finished the largest dam in Africa across it. What the Nile is to Egypt, what the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam changes, why the delta is salting and sinking, and what a city of twenty million does when the only river is spoken for. 22 September 2026 7 min Thirsty Places: Karachi The largest city in Pakistan gets about half the water it needs through pipes, and the other half through a business of tankers, some of them licensed and many of them not, that draws on the same supply and sells it back to the city at ten times the price. What Karachi's water is, why the pipes never caught up, how the tanker economy works, and why the fix that every engineer can draw has been on paper for thirty years. 22 September 2026 7 min Thirsty Places: The Aral Sea The fourth largest lake on Earth was drained in forty years to grow cotton, and the fishing boats of its ports lie in the sand a hundred kilometres from the water. What happened, what it did to the people who lived there, what a dam built in 2005 brought back, and what the largest man made water disaster in history teaches every irrigated valley since. 21 September 2026 7 min Thirsty Places: Beijing The capital of China has less water per person than Saudi Arabia, and for forty years it drank the aquifer beneath it until the ground sank and the wells ran dry. The answer was the largest water transfer ever built, a canal fourteen hundred kilometres long that carries a river from the south to the north. What Beijing's water is, what the transfer cost and delivered, and why a city can be saved by a canal and still be running out. 21 September 2026 7 min Thirsty Places: Cape Town In 2018 a city of four million came within weeks of switching off its taps, and did not. How the drought arrived, what the city did in the last hundred days, what it has built since, and what a near miss taught every other city that watched. 21 September 2026 7 min Thirsty Places: Chennai In June 2019 the four reservoirs of India's sixth largest city held almost nothing, and water arrived by train from two hundred kilometres away. Four years earlier the same city had been under water. What happened to Chennai, why a city with a monsoon runs dry, what the rain barrels on every roof are for, and what the trains and the desalination plants did and did not solve. 21 September 2026 7 min Thirsty Places: Israel A country that is more than half desert grows its own food, exports fruit, and drinks the sea. Most of that comes down to two decisions made in the 1960s: to deliver water to plants a drop at a time, and to send a city's treated sewage to the farms of the Negev. What drip irrigation is, why it was invented where it was, and what a country built on it looks like. 21 September 2026 7 min Thirsty Places: Jakarta The capital of Indonesia is sinking faster than any large city on Earth, up to twenty five centimetres a year in its northern districts, because ten million people pump the ground beneath them for water the pipes do not deliver. Two fifths of the city is already below sea level, a wall is being built along the coast, and the government is moving the capital to another island. What Jakarta's water is, why the pipes failed, and whether a city can stop sinking. 21 September 2026 8 min Thirsty Places: Las Vegas A city of two million people in the driest desert in North America, with fountains on its main street, has cut its river water use by a quarter while its population grew by half. It did it by treating and returning nearly every drop used indoors, by paying people to dig up their lawns, and, in the end, by banning grass. What Las Vegas does with its water, and why it is a better model than its reputation. 21 September 2026 7 min Thirsty Places: Lima Ten million people live in a city where it almost never rains, on a coastal desert between the Pacific and the Andes, drinking a river that is fed by glaciers that are melting away. A million and a half of them have no pipe and buy water from trucks at ten times the price the wealthy pay for theirs. What Lima's water is, where it comes from, what the fog on the hills has to do with it, and why the poorest city district in this series pays the most per litre. 21 September 2026 7 min Thirsty Places: Mexico City A city of twenty two million people built on a drained lake, sinking by up to half a metre a year because it drinks the ground beneath it, and pumping the rest of its water over a mountain range from a hundred kilometres away. What Mexico City's water is, why the pipes leak a third of it, why the wealthy have water and the poor have tankers, and what a city does when the ground itself is the reservoir it is emptying. 21 September 2026 8 min Thirsty Places: The Murray Darling Australia's largest river system grows most of the country's irrigated food, and for a century the states along it took more water than the rivers had. A drought that lasted a decade, a river mouth that had to be dredged to reach the sea, and a million dead fish forced the country to write a plan that buys water back from farmers and gives it to the river. What the Murray Darling Basin Plan is, why it is the world's largest experiment in water markets, and what it has and has not fixed. 21 September 2026 7 min Thirsty Places: Orange County The largest water reuse plant in the world sits in a suburb of Los Angeles and turns the sewage of two and a half million people into drinking water, by way of the ground. How Orange County got there, what the plant does, why the sea was the reason it was built, and what it learned from the city next door that failed. 21 September 2026 7 min Thirsty Places: Phoenix Five million people live in a desert valley that reaches fifty degrees in summer, on a river that stopped reaching the city a century ago, a canal from the Colorado five hundred kilometres long, and an aquifer they have been told, by law, they must not empty. In 2023 the state stopped approving new housing on groundwater in parts of the valley. What Phoenix's water is, why it is better managed than its reputation, and what the Colorado's decline means for the fastest growing city in America. 21 September 2026 7 min Thirsty Places: São Paulo In 2014 the reservoirs of the largest city in the Americas fell so low that the utility pumped the dead water from beneath the intakes, and twenty million people came within weeks of rationing by rota. The rain had failed, and so had a plan. What happened to São Paulo, why a wet country's city ran dry, what the forest upstream had to do with it, and what was built afterwards. 21 September 2026 7 min Thirsty Places: Tehran A capital of nine million people at the foot of a mountain range, living on five dams and an aquifer, in a country that has spent its water on wheat in the desert. The dams fell to a fifth of their capacity, the ground under the southern suburbs is sinking faster than almost anywhere on Earth, and the government has spoken of rationing and of moving the capital. What Tehran's water is, why a country with the mountains ran dry, and what a decade of drought has done. 21 September 2026 7 min Thirsty Places: Windhoek Long before Singapore, the capital of the driest country in sub Saharan Africa began sending its treated sewage straight back to its taps, and has done so since 1968 without a single recorded outbreak. How Windhoek did what no other city dared, what its plant looks like, and why it took the rest of the world fifty years to follow. 21 September 2026 7 min Thirsty Places: Singapore A small island with no rivers, no aquifers and a water supply that arrived across a border decided to make its own. The equipment it chose was ordinary. Persuading a whole country to drink the result was the real engineering. 6 August 2026
