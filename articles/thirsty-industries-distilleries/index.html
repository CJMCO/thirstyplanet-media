Thirsty Industries: Distilleries

A distillery boils a weak beer to collect the alcohol, and for every litre of alcohol it keeps it is left with ten litres or more of what would not boil, a hot, acid, dark liquid a hundred times stronger than sewage. What a distillery uses water for, why a Scotch whisky still needs a cold river more than it needs the water in the bottle, what the dry summers have done on Speyside, what spent wash is and why India ordered its molasses distilleries to discharge none of it, what the tequila towns of Jalisco do with theirs, and why the oldest answer, feeding it to cows, is still one of the best.

12 November 2026 · By Cesar Chu Ortega, founder of Aguato · 8 min read

Share

Every malt whisky distillery in Scotland was built beside running water, and the visitors' tour always explains this with the spring. The water for the mash, the guide says, rises through peat or granite or red sandstone, and gives the whisky its character. There is something in that. Walk round the back of the still house, past the part the tour reaches, and the real reason is in a pipe as wide as a dinner plate that runs from the burn to the building and another that runs back. A still makes vapour, and vapour has to be turned back into liquid, and the only cheap way to do it is to run it through a coil of copper sitting in a great deal of cold water. The spring fills the bottle. The river makes the whisky possible.

This article is about what a distillery does with water: the small amount it keeps, the large amount it borrows to cool, and the difficult amount it leaves behind, which in Scotland is called pot ale, in India spent wash and in Mexico vinaza, and which is one of the strongest waste waters any industry makes.

What a still does

All spirits are made the same way. Something with sugar or starch in it, barley, maize, molasses, grapes, the heart of the agave plant, is mixed with water and fermented with yeast into a weak beer or wine of five to ten percent alcohol. That liquid is heated in a still. Alcohol boils at a lower temperature than water, so the first vapour to rise is rich in it, and when the vapour is cooled the liquid that runs off is stronger than the one that went in. Do it twice in a copper pot, or continuously in a tall column, and the result is a clear spirit of sixty to ninety five percent.

Three kinds of water are involved. The process water goes into the mash and ends up either in the spirit or in the residue. The cooling water passes through the condensers and comes out warm and otherwise unchanged. And at the end, clean water is added to bring the spirit down to the strength on the label, so that sixty percent of a bottle of whisky at forty percent alcohol is water added at the bottling hall.

A Scotch malt distillery, for each litre of pure alcohol
Malted barleyabout 2.5 kilograms
Water in the masharound 10 litres
Cooling water through the condensersmany times that, returned warm
Pot ale left in the first stillabout 8 litres
Draff, the spent grainabout 2.5 kilograms

The borrowed river

The cooling water is by far the largest flow, and almost none of it is consumed: it goes back to the burn or the river a few degrees warmer. That makes it harmless in a wet month and a problem in a dry one, for two reasons. A warm discharge into a low river can raise its temperature enough to harm the salmon, which is why the Scottish regulator sets a limit on the warming, and a distillery cannot take water that is not there.

There are about a hundred and fifty distilleries in Scotland, and about fifty of them stand in the valley of one river, the Spey, and its tributaries. Distilleries have always closed for a few weeks in summer, the silent season, partly for maintenance and partly because the burns ran low. In the summer of 2018, the driest in decades in the east of Scotland, the silence was not planned: several distilleries on Speyside, in Perthshire and on the island of Islay stopped production for weeks because their water sources had fallen too low to cool the stills. The regulator has issued water scarcity warnings for the Spey in several summers since. The industry's response has been to put in cooling towers and closed loops that reuse the same water, which cuts the amount taken from the river and costs electricity.

What is left in the still

When the first distillation is finished, what remains in the pot is everything from the fermented wash that did not boil off: dead yeast, proteins, unfermented sugars, acids, and most of the water. At a malt distillery there are about eight litres of this pot ale for every litre of alcohol produced. Nothing in it is poisonous, and all of it is food, which is the difficulty. Put into a stream, it is eaten by bacteria that use up the oxygen in the water as they eat, and pot ale has a biochemical oxygen demand of twenty five thousand milligrams a litre and more, about a hundred times that of raw sewage. That is why the BOD article on this site lists distillery waste at the top of its scale. Pot ale from copper stills also carries dissolved copper, which limits how much of it can be spread on land.

Scotland's answer is two centuries old. The spent grain, called draff, is sold wet to farmers as cattle feed, and the pot ale is evaporated to a syrup and mixed with it to make dark pellets that are sold as feed across Europe. Distilleries on the coast and the islands were long permitted to pipe pot ale to the sea, where the tide disperses it. The newer answer treats it as fuel: at Rothes, on the Spey, a plant owned jointly by the local distillers burns draff to make electricity, and several of the largest distilleries now feed their pot ale to tanks of bacteria that turn it into gas for the boilers. It is the anaerobic process that the sludge article on this site describes.

Spent wash

The hardest version of the problem is in India. Most Indian alcohol, for drinking and for fuel, is distilled from molasses, the black syrup left when sugar has been crystallised out of cane juice. Molasses carries all the salts and colour of the cane in concentrated form, and what it leaves in the still is correspondingly worse than pot ale. The regulator's figures are ten to fifteen litres of spent wash for each litre of alcohol, coming off the still near boiling, acid, the colour of strong coffee, with an oxygen demand of forty to sixty thousand milligrams a litre and enough potassium to salt a field.

Spent wash from a molasses distillery
Volume10 to 15 litres per litre of alcohol
Biochemical oxygen demand40,000 to 60,000 milligrams per litre
Chemical oxygen demand80,000 to 120,000 milligrams per litre
AciditypH about 4 to 4.5
Raw sewage, for comparisonBOD of 200 to 400 milligrams per litre

For decades it went into lagoons beside the distilleries and from there, in the monsoon, into the rivers, and the reaches of the Ganges and its tributaries below the sugar towns of Uttar Pradesh ran brown in the crushing season. Biological treatment removes most of the oxygen demand and leaves the colour, which is made of the same compounds that brown a crust of bread and which bacteria cannot digest. So in 2003 the central regulator stopped asking for treatment and asked for nothing to leave at all. A molasses distillery in India must now send out no liquid at all, which is the rule behind the zero liquid discharge article on this site. It has two main ways to comply. It can concentrate the spent wash by evaporation and burn the concentrate in its own boiler, which makes steam and power for the plant and leaves an ash rich in potash that is sold as fertiliser. Or it can mix the spent wash into heaps of press mud, the fibrous waste of the sugar mill, and compost it. Enforcement is uneven, and the courts still order distilleries on the Ganges closed, and the rule itself has held for twenty years.

The pressure on it is growing. India is blending ethanol into its petrol, with a target of a fifth, and has licensed hundreds of new distilleries to make it. The government's own roadmap puts the water needed for a litre of ethanol from sugar cane at about 2,860 litres, nearly all of it irrigation in the field. The same point is made by the sugar article on this site, about the same crop in the same dry states.

Tequila

Tequila is distilled from the cooked heart of the blue agave, in a small region of the state of Jalisco, and its residue has the same character as the others and a worse history. A review by Mexican researchers in 2010 found that each litre of tequila left ten to twelve litres of vinaza, at ninety degrees and a pH below four, with an oxygen demand of sixty to a hundred thousand milligrams a litre, and that most of it at that time was going into streams, drains and fields with little or no treatment. Production has roughly doubled since then, to several hundred million litres a year, which means several billion litres of vinaza. The largest producers have built digesters and treatment plants, and the many small distilleries, which lack the money for them, are where the streams around the town of Tequila still run dark.

The bottle

Seen from the kitchen, the largest water in a bottle of spirits is in the crop. A litre of pure alcohol takes about two and a half kilograms of malted barley, and barley carries about 1,420 litres of water a kilogram on the world's average, so a bottle of whisky, which holds a little over a quarter of a litre of pure alcohol, carries something like a thousand litres by this site's arithmetic, nearly all of it rain on a barley field. In Scotland that rain is not short. In the cane fields of Maharashtra the same sum is done with irrigation water.

What it teaches

A distillery is a small user of water with a concentrated problem. What it takes for the product is modest, what it borrows for cooling goes back, and what it leaves in the still is strong enough that a single plant without treatment can kill a river for kilometres. The good news is that the residue is rich: it can be fed to cattle, burned for steam or turned into gas, and every one of those pays some of its own cost. The places where it still reaches a river are the places where nobody has made the distillery choose.

About eight litres left in the still for every litre of whisky, ten to fifteen for molasses, and a burn behind the still house that has to keep running cold.
