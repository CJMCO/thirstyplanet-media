Thirsty Places: London

The city with the rainy reputation gets less rain than Rome, Istanbul or Sydney, stores about a hundred days of water, and loses about a quarter of what it treats through pipes laid when Victoria was queen. Where London's water comes from, why the ground under it stopped falling and started rising, what the Great Stink of 1858 built and what the new tunnel under the Thames has just replaced, why the desalination plant on the estuary has hardly run, what the hosepipe bans of 2012 and 2022 showed, and why a company deep in debt is being asked to find a billion more litres a day by the middle of the century.

12 November 2026 · By Cesar Chu Ortega, founder of Aguato · 8 min read

Share

Anyone who lands at Heathrow from the west on a clear day sees them from the window a minute before the runway: a cluster of enormous rectangular lakes with raised grass banks, right beside the motorway, too regular to be natural and too big to be anything a visitor can easily guess. They are London's water. The Thames is pumped into them upstream of the city, and they hold it for weeks while the mud settles and the sunlight does some of the work of cleaning, before it goes to the treatment works and into the mains. All of them together hold about a hundred days of supply. That is the whole reserve of a city of ten million people, and it explains most of what follows.

This article is about where London's water comes from, why there is less of it than the weather suggests, what the Victorians built and what has just been built to relieve it, and why the city that invented the modern water system is now short of water, pipes and money at the same time.

Less rain than Rome

London's rain is a matter of frequency. It falls on about one day in three, usually lightly, and adds up to about six hundred millimetres in a year, where Rome gets about eight hundred, and Sydney twelve hundred, in fewer and heavier storms. The south east is the driest corner of Britain, and it is also the most crowded. Divide the rain by the people and the region has less water for each of them than several countries around the Mediterranean, a comparison the government's environment agency makes itself, and the agency classes the area as seriously water stressed.

About two thirds of the supply is taken from the Thames, above the point where the tide reaches, and from the smaller river Lee in the north east. The other third is pumped from the chalk, the soft white limestone that lies under the city like a saucer and comes to the surface in the hills to the north and south. Both sources depend on the same thing, which is winter. Summer rain in England mostly evaporates or is taken up by plants. It is the slow rain from October to March that soaks into the chalk and keeps the rivers flowing through the following summer, so that one dry winter is a concern and two in a row are a drought.

The ground that came back

When the fountains of Trafalgar Square were built in the 1840s, they were supplied from wells sunk into the chalk a short walk away. Over the next hundred years the breweries, paper mills, hotels and department stores of central London drilled their own wells and pumped, and the water table fell and kept falling, until in the 1960s it stood nearly ninety metres below sea level in the middle of the basin.

Then the industry left. As the factories closed and the offices went onto mains water, the pumping stopped, and the chalk began to refill, at up to three metres a year in places. The sinking cities on this site would envy that, and it brought a problem of its own. The underground railway tunnels, the deep basements and the foundations of the towers had all been built in ground that was dry at the time, and rising water threatened to flood the first and soften the clay around the others. Since the 1990s the water company, the underground and the environment agency have run a programme that does something odd for a city short of water: it pumps the chalk on purpose, to hold the level down, and puts what it pumps into the supply. That is the opposite of the Jakarta and Mexico City articles on this site, where the ground sinks because the pumping never stopped. London shows what happens when it does.

The Great Stink

The other half of the city's water story is what it did with its waste. Until the middle of the nineteenth century London's sewers ran straight into the Thames, and several of the water companies drew their supply from the same reach of river. What followed is in the cholera article on this site, with the doctor in Soho who traced an outbreak to one pump in 1854. Parliament had already ordered the companies to move their intakes above the tide and to filter the water through sand, a method first used at a works in Chelsea in 1829 and still in use in London today.

What finally moved the government was a smell. In the hot summer of 1858 the river, low and full of sewage, stank so badly that the curtains of the Houses of Parliament were soaked in chloride of lime and the members debated moving out. Within weeks they had given the engineer Joseph Bazalgette the money he had been asking for. Over the next sixteen years he built a hundred and thirty kilometres of great brick sewers running from west to east along both sides of the river, which caught the old sewers before they reached it and carried everything by gravity to outfalls far downstream, and he hid the ones beside the river under the new embankments that still carry its roads and gardens. He sized the pipes for a population well above the three million then living in the city, and that margin is why they lasted.

They carry rain as well as sewage, which is the arrangement the storm overflows article on this site explains, and with ten million people and far more paved ground the margin ran out. By the early years of this century the sewers were spilling into the river about once a week, some thirty nine million tonnes of diluted sewage a year. The answer took a decade to build and was fully connected in 2025: a tunnel twenty five kilometres long and seven metres across, as much as sixty six metres under the bed of the Thames, which catches the overflows and holds them until the works downstream can treat them. It cost about five billion pounds, added to every customer's bill.

A quarter gone

The mains are the same age as the sewers. London has about thirty two thousand kilometres of water pipe, and roughly half of it is more than a hundred years old: cast iron, laid in the London clay, which swells when it is wet and shrinks when it is dry and so bends the pipes a little twice a year. Cast iron does not bend. In a hard frost or after a long dry summer the bursts come in hundreds.

Thames Water puts about 2,600 million litres into supply each day and loses roughly a quarter of it, somewhere around six hundred million litres, enough for about four million people. Compare the Tokyo article on this site, about a city that brought the same figure down from a fifth to three percent by replacing its mains over sixty years. London began a replacement programme in the 2000s, was slowed by the cost and by the disruption of digging up some of the busiest streets in Europe, and has missed its leakage targets in several years since.

The household end is not much better measured. England was slow to meter water, and many London homes still pay a flat charge that does not depend on what they use, which the water meters article on this site covers. A Londoner uses about a hundred and forty litres a day.

Dry years

In the spring of 2012, after two dry winters, the reservoirs and the chalk were low enough that seven water companies in the south east banned the use of hosepipes, weeks before the city was due to hold the Olympic Games. It then rained for three months, one of the wettest summers on record, and the ban was lifted. In the summer of 2022 the temperature in England passed forty degrees for the first time, the source of the Thames dried up and the river began several kilometres further down its valley, and the hosepipe ban returned and stayed until November.

Neither drought emptied a tap. Both showed how little lies between the city and a real shortage: a hundred days of stored river, a chalk aquifer that needs wet winters, and no large new source since the 1970s. A desalination plant was built on the estuary at Beckton and opened in 2010, able on paper to supply a hundred and fifty million litres a day by treating the brackish water of the outgoing tide. It has run rarely and below that figure, and during the drought of 2022 it was out of service.

A billion litres

The company's own plan says that by 2050, with more people, hotter summers and less water allowed to be taken from chalk streams that are drying up, its region will be short by about a billion litres a day, more than a third of what it supplies now. About half of the gap is meant to be closed by fixing leaks and by meters. For the rest there is a reservoir near Abingdon, upstream in Oxfordshire, that has been proposed in one form or another since the 1990s and was approved in principle in the 2020s, holding a hundred and fifty million cubic metres and costing several billion pounds; and a scheme to take more water from the Thames at Teddington and replace it with highly treated effluent from the sewage works nearby. That is the idea behind the Singapore article on this site.

The difficulty is who pays. The water industry of England was sold to private owners in 1989. Thames Water's owners over the following decades loaded it with debt, which now stands in the high teens of billions of pounds, and paid themselves dividends while the pipes aged. The regulator has fined the company repeatedly for leaks and for sewage spills, including penalties of more than a hundred million pounds in 2025, and the company has spent the middle of this decade negotiating with its lenders and the government over its own rescue. The reservoir, the mains and the sewage works all have to be paid for by the same customers, through the same bills.

What it teaches

London is the place on this site where the water system was invented and then left alone. The sand filter, the intercepting sewer, the reservoir beside the river and the idea that a city owes its people clean water all date from here, between 1829 and 1875, and they were built with so much margin that the city lived on them for a century and a half. The margin is used up. There is no shortage of engineering answers, and one of them, the tunnel, has just been finished. What is short is what Tokyo had and London did not: sixty years of steady spending on things under the ground.

Six hundred millimetres of rain, a hundred days in the reservoirs, and a quarter of the water gone before it reaches a tap.
