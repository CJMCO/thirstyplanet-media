Thirsty: Apple

One apple carries about 125 litres of water, most of it rain on an orchard, which makes it one of the gentler things in the fruit bowl. Where an apple's water goes, why the tree needs it at the wrong time of year in the places that grow the best fruit, how the largest orchard region in America lives on melting snow and what happened in the year the snow did not come, why growers in the Alps spray their blossom with water on freezing nights, what the orchards of China's loess hills are doing to the soil beneath them, and why a glass of juice carries the water of two apples and none of the fibre.

12 November 2026 · By Cesar Chu Ortega, founder of Aguato · 8 min read

Share

On a clear night in April in the Adige valley, in the Italian Alps, when the forecast says the temperature will fall below freezing before dawn, the apple growers do something that looks from the road like a mistake. They turn on the sprinklers. All night, water falls on trees that are in full blossom, and by morning every branch, every flower and every wire of the trellis is inside a sleeve of clear ice, and the orchard glitters like glass for an hour before the sun takes it apart. The flowers inside the ice are alive. Water gives up heat as it freezes, and as long as fresh water keeps arriving and freezing on the outside, the blossom at the centre stays at about zero, which it can survive, while the air around it falls to five or six degrees below, which would kill it and the year's crop with it.

That is one of the things an apple's water is for. One apple carries about 125 litres, and this article is about where those litres go, why the best apple country in the world is too dry to grow apples without help, what happened in the year the snow failed in Washington, and why the juice is the heavier way to eat the fruit.

The number

On the Water Footprint Network's figures, a kilogram of apples carries about 822 litres of water on the world's average, so an apple of 150 grams carries about 125. Two thirds of that is green water, rain that fell on the orchard and was drawn up through the tree. About a sixth is blue water, irrigation from a river or a well, and about a sixth is grey water, the volume needed to dilute the fertiliser and the sprays that leave the orchard.

Among fruit that is a modest figure. The banana article on this site puts a banana at about 100 litres and the avocado article puts one avocado at about 400, most of it pumped. The apple's advantage is the tree. An orchard gives thirty to sixty tonnes of fruit a hectare in the good regions, more than almost any other fruit crop, so a season's water is shared among a great many apples.

The world grows about ninety five million tonnes of apples a year, and about half of them grow in China. The rest of this article is about three of the places where the other numbers come from.

Snow in a dry valley

An apple gets its colour and its sugar from warm sunny days and cool nights in the weeks before picking, and it stays free of the fungus diseases that spoil it where the air is dry. The climate that offers all of that is a dry one, and the best example is the Yakima valley in Washington State, on the eastern side of the Cascade mountains, where the mountains take the rain out of the Pacific air before it arrives. The valley gets about two hundred millimetres a year, which is desert by most definitions. Together with the neighbouring Wenatchee and Columbia valleys it grows about two thirds of the apples of the United States.

It does that on snow. The winter storms that miss the valley fall on the Cascades, the snow melts through the spring and early summer, and five reservoirs built by the federal government in the first half of the twentieth century hold the melt and release it into canals through the growing season. A mature orchard there needs about a metre of water over a summer, five times what the sky provides, and some of it is used for a purpose that has nothing to do with thirst: on the hottest afternoons growers run a fine spray over the trees to cool the fruit by evaporation, because an apple in direct sun at forty degrees gets sunburn, a brown patch that makes it unsaleable.

The system has a weakness that the winter of 2015 exposed. The storms came that year as usual, and they came warm, so that what fell on the mountains was rain, which ran off in January and was gone, where snow would have waited until June. By spring the snowpack stood at a small fraction of normal. Water rights in the American West follow the order in which they were claimed, and the irrigation districts whose rights date from 1905 or later are cut first: that summer they received 47 percent of their water. One district shut its main canal for three weeks in May to save water for the hottest months. Growers pulled out older orchards to keep the younger ones alive, and the state counted the losses in hundreds of millions of dollars. An orchard cannot be left unplanted for a year like a wheat field; it is the same problem the almonds article on this site describes, a tree that needs its water every year for twenty five years in a climate that no longer promises it.

The valley's answer, agreed in 2013 between the farmers, the Yakama Nation, the towns and the fishing interests, is a thirty year plan of more storage, lined canals, water markets and fish passages. It is being built, slowly, while the winters get warmer.

Ice on the blossom

South Tyrol, the German speaking province in the north of Italy where this article began, grows about a tenth of the apples of the European Union on eighteen thousand hectares of valley floor and lower slope. It looks wet, with snow peaks on every side, and its valleys are among the driest in the Alps: the Vinschgau, the upper part of the Adige valley, lies in the rain shadow of the mountains around it and gets about five hundred millimetres a year. For seven hundred years its farmers carried meltwater along the slopes in open channels cut into the rock, and some of those channels still run.

The orchards are irrigated for two reasons, and frost is the famous one. A sprinkler system sized to protect blossom has to put down three or four millimetres of water an hour over the whole orchard, which is thirty or forty cubic metres on each hectare every hour for as long as the frost lasts, and in a cold spring there may be ten such nights. Much of that water runs through the soil and back to the river, so it is borrowed more than used, though it has to be available all at once, on the same night, to every grower in the valley, which is why the province has built reservoirs for the purpose. For summer watering the growers have been moving from overhead sprinklers to drip lines along the rows, which use about half as much.

The loess hills

China's apples grow mostly in two regions, the Shandong peninsula on the coast and the Loess Plateau inland, in Shaanxi and Gansu provinces, where orchards have spread since the 1980s across hills of deep, soft, yellow soil. The plateau gets five or six hundred millimetres of rain a year, mostly in late summer, and most of its orchards are not irrigated. They live on rain and on the water stored in the loess, which is many metres deep and holds water like a sponge.

Soil scientists who drill into those hills have found that under an orchard of fifteen or twenty years the sponge has been emptied. The trees' roots go down ten metres and more, and they take out more each year than the rain puts back, so that a dry layer forms deep in the soil and does not refill. The orchard keeps bearing as long as the rains are good. The cost appears later, when the old trees are pulled out and the next crop finds nothing stored below, and in the streams of the plateau, which are fed by water that used to pass through the soil. It is a quiet version of the story the groundwater article on this site tells, a store spent faster than it fills, and it does not show in the footprint at all, because all of it counts as rain.

The glass of juice

A litre of apple juice carries about 1,140 litres of water, and a glass of 200 millilitres about 230, close to the water of two apples. The arithmetic is simple: it takes about a kilogram and a third of apples to press a litre, and the pulp and peel that are left take a small share of the water with them as animal feed. It is the same finding as the orange juice article on this site. Juice is the fruit with the fibre taken out, and it is drunk in a few seconds where the apples would have taken ten minutes to eat.

Then there is the apple that is never eaten. Around a third of the fruit grown in the world is lost between the tree and the plate, to blemishes at the packing house, to storage and to the bottom of the fruit bowl at home. An apple thrown away has used its 125 litres all the same.

What it teaches

The apple is a fruit with a moderate footprint, mostly rain, and a tree that gives a great deal for what it drinks. Its water story is about timing more than amount. The climate that makes the best apples is dry when the tree is thirsty, so the great orchard regions depend on water stored somewhere else: as snow in the Cascades, as meltwater in an Alpine reservoir, as old rain deep in the loess. Each of those stores is changing. Snow is falling as rain, glaciers are shrinking, and the loess is being drawn down, and an orchard planted this year will still need its water in 2050.

For the person with the fruit bowl, the apple is one of the easier choices on this site. Eat it whole, eat the one with the mark on it, and it is hard to do better.

About 125 litres an apple, two thirds of it rain, and a night in April when the sprinklers run to keep the blossom alive.
